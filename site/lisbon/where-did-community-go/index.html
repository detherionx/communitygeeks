Where did community go?

By Carmelito Bauer

966 questions a month, and why the decline started long before AI. Part one of two ahead of Lisbon Community Week.

In March 2014, people asked 207,169 new questions on Stack Overflow. In September 2026, they asked 966.

I counted both months through the Stack Exchange API at the end of October, because a number like that deserves checking. Others got the same result. People still read the site: in Stack Overflow's 2026 developer survey, 78% of visitors said they come to read. Almost nobody asks.

The easy explanation is ChatGPT, and it explains part of it. When researchers compared Stack Overflow with developer communities on Reddit, Stack Overflow lost about 12% of its daily visitors within months of ChatGPT's launch, and newer users were the most likely to leave. The Reddit communities showed no decline. The authors put the difference down to social fabric: people stayed where they had relationships.

The line was falling long before that. Between March 2014 and September 2020, two years before ChatGPT existed, monthly questions dropped by about a third, from 207,169 to 141,728. In 2025, The Pragmatic Engineer traced the start of the decline to 2014, when the site began closing questions faster and many newcomers stopped feeling welcome. A study in PNAS Nexus that measured the ChatGPT effect also documents a decline reaching back to 2016.

Other communities tell the same story with even less AI in it. In May, Sephora announced it would retire its Beauty Insider Community forum, one of the best-known brand communities of the last decade. The forum was the last piece of an older setup. Sephora had been paying creators through Sephora Squad since 2019, and in September 2025 it gave creators storefronts inside its own app that earn commission. Reviews, Q&A and the loyalty programme stayed. The open forum is the part it let go. In June, the makers of RPG Maker said their official forums would go read-only and then offline in December: nearly fifteen years of tutorials and help, 1.4 million posts. Vonage now tells developers to ask its AI assistant first, and reports a 93% self-service score for it. Its community work has moved into a partner programme of meetups, workshops and hackathons run by local developer groups.

The forum's slow decline has its own literature. In 2011 the New York Times already called the internet message board an endangered species. Communities moved to Facebook groups, then subreddits, then Discord servers, because joining one more space there cost a click instead of a new account. Discord made the cost visible: help channels full of answers no search engine could find. In 2022 one developer counted more than eight million messages in the help channels of just nine Discord communities, none of them searchable. Last week Discord announced it will publish summaries of its public servers on the web from 16 November, with selected messages in search results from December.

Inside companies, the measurement problem is just as old. Community teams were judged on posts, members and active users, and practitioners kept warning that those numbers say little about value. In 2015 the Community Roundtable told teams to measure "a valuable community, as opposed to a popular website". In 2022 Rosie Sherry called community engagement "the new growth hacking". This July, an essay in Community Inc ran under the title "Engagement Was Always a Proxy".

The 2026 CMX Community Industry Report shows where that leaves the profession. 93% of community professionals now use AI tools, up from 81% a year earlier. In the same survey, the share who can confidently quantify community's business value fell from 24% to 17%. Proving ROI is their biggest budget challenge, named by 41%.

AI sped up something that was already happening. The container got weaker, and the activity numbers we used to defend it had never measured what people actually got out of it.

When I worked with DeepL, I saw priorities shift the same way, away from running one big enterprise community. At the same time, interest grew in the people building with the product. Developers put the API into their own tools. Partners built products on top of it. Most of them never posted in a forum, and they were doing a lot.

At the end of 2024, Anthropic released the Model Context Protocol, a standard for connecting AI models to other tools. About a year later there were more than 10,000 active public servers built on it, by companies and developers everywhere. Hugging Face, where people share AI models, passed three million models in August.

At OutSystems, a low-code platform, a developer built a component called Ultimate PDF that turns web pages into reports and invoices. Other components now depend on it. When a platform upgrade broke it, the help came from its creator and from peers in the forum.

Check out our Ultimate PDF case study.

At CD PROJEKT RED, one of the leads on Cyberpunk 2 estimates that about half of his quest team are former modders. He calls modding "a gateway into game dev".

Very little of this shows up as posts in a company forum. It shows up in a product, a hiring pipeline, a list of dependencies. It is participation around the product, and most community dashboards miss it.

So how do you look at your own community, if counting posts tells you so little? Next week I'll publish the six questions we use, and on 13 November I'll walk through them on stage at Lisbon Community Week.
