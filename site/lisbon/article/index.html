We’re speaking at the same conference. Are we doing the same job?

By Carmelito Bauer

Four questions about what people build, and who supports it, ahead of Lisbon Community Week.

I’m going to Lisbon Community Week to give a talk called “The Death of Community Management”. There are probably easier ways to introduce yourself to a room full of community managers. Looking at the other speakers’ work, though, the interesting question is how much responsibility that job can cover.

My argument is that technology companies need to understand themselves as builder ecosystems. People outside the company extend its products, teach others, build businesses around them and form relationships the company could never organise individually. Supporting what those people want to build becomes part of the job, including when it doesn’t match the company’s next marketing request. Four people coming to Lisbon give me different ways into that argument.

At OutSystems, the building is quite literal. Forge is its library of reusable components. Community Engagement Manager Andreia Tulcidás’s announcement of the 2025 NEO Awards recognises contributions including downloaded components, forum solutions and training. The Champions programme includes maintaining components. That is where I start paying attention. Imagine publishing something useful and discovering that other people’s projects depend on you keeping it working. A contribution can become a responsibility you never quite signed up for. OutSystems offers Champions mentoring, training and access to teams to help them develop. I’m interested in what support exists when they need to step back.

Andreia, if the maintainer of a widely used Forge component wanted to walk away tomorrow, what would happen to the people building on it, and who should take responsibility?

Evan Hamilton, formerly Director of Community at HubSpot, has written about several departments recruiting the same helpful customers into separate programmes. He argues for central coordination, with “air traffic control” among its benefits: making sure the highest-priority requests come first. I picture being on the receiving end: by the time everyone has finished appreciating you, you have several newsletters and more things to do. HubSpot’s Community Champions programme supports answering questions and sharing content; its FAQ says HubFans and Community Champions were brought under the same name. Here, someone’s contribution might be the explanation that lets another customer finally use a product properly. They might also be building their own reputation or business. Taking that ambition seriously means making choices about what the company asks of them.

Evan, when would you turn down a useful request from a HubSpot team because it gets in the way of what the contributor is trying to build?

Recognising all these possibilities can leave a community team with an absurdly long to-do list. Jennifer Hanley, now Head of Community at Moveworks, announced a refreshed community in 2025 that included customer stories alongside a job and consulting network. A relationship around using a product could develop into paid work between participants. Her Lisbon session, “The Do-Everything Community Trap”, asks how to choose a focus. I want to hear those two things together. A builder ecosystem can create opportunities beyond the company’s immediate priorities, and useful activity might move somewhere the community team cannot see. Deciding whether to support it requires a view of success that extends beyond activity on your own platform.

Jennifer, if two members met through the consulting network, started working together and stopped posting, what would you need to know to count that as a success worth investing in?

Terra Milo, who hosts Lisbon Game Night, describes a less hypothetical problem with deciding what belongs to whom. In her February 2025 account, the café owner’s Meetup account had an unspecified problem, leaving the group without an organiser. She says someone took over, deleted its events and announced only two games at his bar, replacing the variety at their usual café. He had the admin permissions. The five years of Tuesdays were another matter. Terra describes members objecting; she told him they had rebuilt the community before and could do it again. After negotiations, he relinquished the group and she paid to become its organiser. A game night is hardly a software ecosystem, but the ownership assumption travels: providing infrastructure doesn’t give you unlimited authority over what people have built together.

Terra, you told him you could rebuild the community again. What did recovering the existing Meetup group preserve that starting again would have cost you?

So, are we doing the same job? We share responsibility for helping people do things together, but maintaining dependencies between developers, coordinating a company’s demands, choosing which relationships to support and hosting a weekly gathering call for different decisions. Builder ecosystems make those differences consequential: the people contributing have projects and ambitions of their own, and others may depend on their work. Community work becomes difficult when that responsibility extends to things the organisation doesn’t own or control.

These are questions drawn from public material, with the answers still to come. If you have an example that complicates the comparison, I’d like to hear it, especially one where helping people build meant changing what the company wanted from them.
