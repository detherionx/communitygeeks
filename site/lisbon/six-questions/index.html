Six questions about the people around your product

By Carmelito Bauer

A lens for looking at participation beyond your own platform. Part two of three ahead of Lisbon Community Week.

Last week I wrote about where community went: Stack Overflow’s 966 questions a month, Sephora retiring its forum, and the work that keeps growing around products somewhere else. The question I hear most after conversations like that is a fair one: what do I do with this on Monday?

Most teams already have part of an answer. Community answers feed the support bot. Unanswered questions become tickets. Customer success sees which accounts are struggling. That’s real value, and much of the old community function lives on in it. It still only covers what customers ask and answer inside the company’s own channels.

To see the rest, we use six questions. We call it the Participation Lens.

The Participation Lens.

Six questions about the people around your product.

Customers, admins, developers, creators and partners, and often your own employees, who answer, post and run events too. So do ambassadors and superusers, fans and players, consultants and agencies. Count the quiet people who read everything and rarely post.

Most people arrive to learn or get help. Many stay to connect with peers, shape the product or build something of their own. Others come to be recognised, to earn, to help others or for the fun of it.

Answers and posts are the visible part. Feedback and bug reports were what practitioners named most, followed by referrals and videos. Behind them sit guides, templates, code and integrations, and the events people run themselves.

On your platform and inside the product, and in Discord, Slack, LinkedIn, YouTube, GitHub, Reddit, marketplaces, at conferences and in other people’s communities. Real-time chat loses answers fast, so the next person asking finds no trail.

Other customers, partners and your product team, and more and more support, sales, customer success, marketing and AI answer engines. If it only reaches a dashboard, that is the part AI absorbs first.

Someone has to keep it working when it breaks, when its maker leaves, when your own champion leaves, or when your requests clash with theirs. Ask whose budget it sits in and who answers the questions nobody picks up.

Put the lens to work.

Take the six questions into your next product, community or ecosystem discussion.

The first four describe what people already do, and you can often answer them from the outside. The last two decide whether any of it lasts.

LEGO Ideas

Since 2008, LEGO has let fans design their own sets. Run it through the six questions:

  • Who takes part: fans who design sets.
  • Why: to see their own design on the shelf, for the credit, and for a royalty of 1% of net sales plus ten copies of the set.
  • What they make: complete set designs.
  • Where it happens: on LEGO’s Ideas site, and wherever designers go to gather the 10,000 supporters an idea needs to enter one of three yearly reviews.
  • Who uses it: LEGO’s designers develop the winners together with the fan, and people buy the result. The Saturn V rocket, the typewriter and the tree house all started this way. The first, a research submarine called Shinkai 6500, shipped in 2011.
  • Who looks after it: LEGO does. It runs the review, redesigns the set and pays the royalty.

LEGO is a toy company. People outside a company making things that others use is not a software phenomenon.

Your own community

Now run the same questions on your own community. If the answer to “what do they make” is mostly posts, and the answer to “who uses it” is mostly a dashboard, you are looking at the part AI absorbs first. If the answer includes templates, events, integrations and answers that other customers build on, you already run a builder ecosystem, whatever your job title says.

Run the support-and-CRM model from the start of this piece through them too. It answers the first three questions well. Ask it who else makes something around your product, where that happens and who looks after it, and most of the answers stay blank.

The same test answers the objection that all this only applies to AI and software companies. Sephora sells beauty products, and it retired its forum after years of building creator programmes that now earn commission inside its own app. LEGO makes toys. At CD PROJEKT RED, one of the leads on Cyberpunk 2 estimates that about half of his quest team are former modders. A big forum is one place where people make things around a product, and often no longer the main one.

Who looks after it

At OutSystems, a developer built Ultimate PDF, a component that turns web pages into reports and invoices, and other components now depend on it. When a platform upgrade broke it, the help came from its creator and from peers. That worked, and it also shows how much can rest on one person. When thousands of people depend on something someone maintains in their free time, who is responsible if that person walks away?

Most teams I talk to can’t answer that yet. I put a version of it to Andreia Tulcidás from OutSystems ahead of Lisbon, and I expect it to come up more than once that week.

Start with what people already do around your product, then decide what to support.

The Lens is on communitygeeks.ai/lisbon as a one-page download, next to the questions I wrote down for fellow speakers. On 13 November I’ll walk through it at Lisbon Community Week. If you run it on your own community and something doesn’t fit, I’d like to hear about it.
