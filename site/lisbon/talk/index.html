The Death of Community Management

Why that’s good news.

Lisbon Community Week · 13 November 2026 · Carmelito Bauer

This is the talk as I wrote it. On the day I went off script in a few places, so treat this as the version I meant to give.

Download the slides (PDF)

PDF · 19 slides · 3.6 MB

Edith

1911. A small town on the James River in Virginia: Scottsville.

This is Edith Taggart. She was thirteen years old, and Scottsville called her “Central.” “Number, please?” She knew where everyone was: the doctor’s house, the hardware store. Some people called just to hear her voice.

She did that for almost forty years. In 1952 Scottsville got dial telephones, and the town operator disappeared.

The switchboard disappeared. Making phone calls, and connecting with other people, didn’t. People made more calls than ever, to more people than Edith could ever have reached.

Today I want to argue that something similar is happening to what we call community management.

The places built for asking are emptying

Sephora retired its Beauty Insider Community forum, announced in May 2026. Reddit’s CEO said Google’s AI Mode “does not drive traffic.” Stack Overflow had 207,169 new questions in March 2014. In September 2026 it had 966. People still read it. Almost nobody asks.

Not all of that is caused by AI.

The old scoreboard

And inside our companies, most of us are still counting the same things: tickets deflected, active members, event attendance, posts and page views.

Optimising these numbers is what I’ve started calling containerslop: any plan for the future of community that only improves the container.

containerslop (n.)

Any plan for the future of community that only improves the container.

Tickets deflected, active members, event attendance, posts and page views. The work people build around the product never shows up in them.

Most fixes polish the container

Here’s what it looks like. Make the forum AI-ready, so the support bot can answer from the old threads. Pipe the community into the CRM, so sales sees churn risk. Add a “how did you hear about us?” field to prove pipeline.

All useful, and I’ve done some of them myself. None of them sees a maintainer keeping an integration alive, or an ambassador running a hackathon.

Making got cheap

So, is this the end of the line?

This is where things get interesting. Making got cheap. In one year, 36 million new developers joined GitHub, and most new ones use an AI assistant in their first week. In games, 3,670 new titles came out on Steam in October 2026, 80% more than a year earlier. Fewer people ask. Far more people build.

In my own client work, priorities moved away from just having an enterprise community, and interest grew in the people building with the product. So I set out to research this.

People now build around the product

What I found was participation around the product: people making things with it, often outside any forum.

AI companies call this a builder ecosystem. Public MCP servers passed 10,000 about a year after launch. In software, the OutSystems Forge holds more than 8,900 components shared by its developer community, many of them not supported by OutSystems itself. And it goes beyond IT: 79 LEGO sets have been designed by fans since 2014.

The Participation Lens

So how do you look at this in your own work? We use six questions. We call it the Participation Lens.

Who takes part? Why do they do it? What do they make? Where does it happen? Who uses it? Who looks after it?

LEGO Ideas

Let’s run the six questions on one example first.

Fans take part. They do it to see their own design on the shelf, and for one percent of net sales. They make sets. It happens on LEGO’s site, and wherever designers find the 10,000 supporters they need for a review. LEGO’s designers turn the idea into a product that people buy. And LEGO looks after it: a review, a redesign, the royalty back to the fan.

What are people trying to do together?

Now run the same questions on your own community. The question shifts from “How do we get more engagement?” to “What are people trying to do together?”

If the answer to “what do they make” is posts, and “who uses it” is a dashboard, that’s the part AI is absorbing first. If the answer is templates, events, integrations and answers other customers build on, you’re already doing this work, whatever your job title says.

Some of you will say you already do this, and that participation has always crossed teams. That’s true. One practitioner put it well: it was always there, but now it’s formalised. What’s new is that people make things around your product, and making got cheap.

Same skills. New scoreboard.

So what would we count instead?

You still run support, onboarding, events and content. Those skills stay. What changes is what you count. Tickets deflected becomes customers unblocked. Active members becomes members who make something. Event attendance becomes events members run themselves. Posts and page views becomes things others reuse, from templates and guides to recipes, mods and integrations.

Same people. Wider jobs.

And when the scoreboard changes, so does the job. Four stories from our interviews.

One person had a community manager title and was running moderation, beta tests, onboarding and the ambassadors who drove upsell. When they left, it took about a year to replace them, and the work ended up split across three to four people.

Another had their job description rewritten four months in, because the job had already outgrown it: developer advocacy, templates and hackathons on top of the community.

A third had one community title and six jobs: client success, education, support, partners, marketing and upsell signals.

And the person from the first story, at their next company, got a title for the whole job from day one: Head of Ecosystem.

Who is responsible?

That first story leads to the question I keep asking. It’s the last question of the Lens, who looks after it, and the one most of us can’t answer yet.

When thousands of people depend on something one person maintains in their free time, who is responsible if that person walks away?

Edith’s switchboard disappeared. The need she served kept growing.

Close

There’s more to build together. If you’ve seen this happening, I’d love to hear it.

And if you think I’m wrong: trial by combat. Come find me.
