The End of the Line

Builder ecosystems at AI companies show how community management is moving beyond engagement metrics to developers, partners and contributions that build lasting product value.

Observation · 15 Oct 2026 · Carmelito Bauer

Community Management is fading to black. I have kept reading about layoffs, about engagement numbers going down, even about people advising to look into other career paths. Some of the most famous online communities have shut down entirely, community platform vendors have imploded and AI seems to decrease the number of people even entering communities rather dramatically. This all seems pretty bleak. Maybe a bit too bleak.

Because at the frontier of AI, the opposite is happening. Builders are testing models, reporting edge cases, hosting meetups and writing tutorials around products they don't work for. Companies such as Anthropic and OpenAI are working out what to do with all that contribution. Over the past months I have looked closely at this space and spoken to people doing the work. This piece is about what I found: why traditional community management deserves its obituary, what is replacing it, and the one question every company with builders around its product should be asking.

Faster, Harder, and Louder

Anthropic and OpenAI have built some of the most sophisticated and, frankly, spectacular community programs I have seen lately. Whenever I open LinkedIn or X I see new Anthropic community meetups popping up in the most remote corners of the world. OpenAI hosted a few community meetups near my home. Both of these frontier companies host frequent hackathons and they have developed a comprehensive global developer ecosystem and community with ambassadors, open-source support and events. One of the most impressive initiatives was the OpenAI 2026 Build Week, showcasing some impressive projects Codex users from all corners of the world built.

Nothing about what these frontier companies are building suggests the work itself is dying. In fact, quite the opposite seems to be true: Full steam ahead.

I recently met my mentee Wishmi who co-organises Cursor meetups in Sri Lanka. The whole AI and AI infrastructure industry seems to try to accomplish something very similar, and this is true for the smaller to medium sized startups as well. These are particularly interesting, because here resources have to be allocated differently.

I have taken a close look at what is happening in that space. In my upcoming Lisbon talk I am arguing that traditional “Community Management” is on its deathbed. What these companies are doing is something new, but it is still very much community. It is much closer to what we would probably call a specialised ecosystem: Developers who are building, partners who are integrating, experts who are shaping the product, organisers who are convening and contributors who are creating reusable value around the product.

I am saying: This is what community work is turning into.

Beyond the Realms of Death

So, what does community work look like at the frontier of AI and AI infrastructure companies and startups? The first thing I have observed a lot of them do is the obvious thing: Providing credits, startup support and technical help. But what I saw next was a lot more relevant. These companies have started to give builders an active role in their system.

Nebius serves as a good example. They combine credits with office hours, courses, showcases and hackathons. They call this their Nebius AI Builder Program and notably it includes a Discord community. Builders are getting a role in the Nebius ecosystem, as Nebius calls AI “a system you build.” They make the role that the community plays even more concrete: “AI is built by many teams, tested by the community, and improved by anyone with a better idea”. Nebius Fellows adds another layer to their ecosystem, it is a lot closer to a partnership with active builders, where they are expected to contribute by hosting meetups, create tutorials, showcase open source projects and more. The program states: “We care more about real builders, meaningful contributions, and strong communities than inflated numbers.”

What’s clear here is that a builder ecosystem is more than the sum of its parts. It is not about cheaper access or the community container (Discord, etc.) alone. It gives people a reason and a concrete route to contribute something back. I see most AI companies heading in that direction, albeit from different angles. Together AI adds engineering support, peer events and GTM help around its startup program. Berget is even more explicit: its open-source program gives maintainers tokens, technical guidance, promotion and recognition. Novita mixes startup support with hackathons and benchmark contributions, so builders are not only consuming infrastructure but producing something the wider ecosystem can reuse.

Go With The Flow

Over the past weeks I have looked into and spoken to many leaders on the AI frontier. While many have started to build these kinds of communities and ecosystems, some have stated challenges. And even more importantly, all have acknowledged that builders are already participating and a community forming, the question is if the structure around it is mature enough.

Mistral is further along, but faces the next version of the problem. Jen Person, Head of Developer Relations at Mistral, told me that as more support has moved from publicly indexed spaces into Discord, useful answers can become “pretty ephemeral” and eventually disappear into the history of the conversation, leaving “no trail” for the next person with the same question. The problem here isn’t getting people to participate. It is making the knowledge created through that participation durable.

Community platform vendors are starting to notice the same issues. Discourse observed this in their last blog article: “they do far more than just ‘run a community.’ They’re using Discourse to actively collaborate. Their members share what they've built, trade workflows, test new releases, and directly shape what the company builds next.” Bettermode, another community platform, is starting to think beyond the container too. In a September conversation, Katrina Hansen talked about community as one part of a broader ecosystem, with AI helping bring the right people into conversations and community knowledge feeding back into product, marketing and partnerships.

For Whom the Bell Tolls?

Traditional community management deserves its obituary. The legacy JD and its playbooks have lost much of their appeal. What follows may be a bit counterintuitive, but I am arguing the work is expanding, and I hope I make some of my readers as optimistic as I am about the space. We are no longer building a channel and trying to keep it active. We are figuring out who builds, shapes, teaches, connects and leads around a product. We are creating the conditions so these contributions can accumulate.

The craft has never been as professionalised and skill based as what I see these frontier companies building. Word on this may not have reached other industries quite yet, but I argue it will. Some may now call it ecosystems, devrel, community, participation or any mix of it, but the broader field seems to be heading in the same direction. This means, as community professionals, we have more to learn, but also a lot more useful work to do.

A useful place to start is to look at what people already contribute around your product and ask which of those contributions get lost instead of becoming useful to someone else.

We are not quite done yet.
