What do you do if idea-to-product takes minutes?

AI-made games, modding and fan co-creation show how trust, shared knowledge and participation build lasting value around products as production gets cheaper.

Observation · 8 Oct 2026 · Carmelito Bauer

Open question: “What accumulates rather than resets when spending stops?”

The other day I was doomscrolling Linkedin again, and I stumbled upon a post where someone made a World of Warcraft clone. That’s a game I used to play quite a lot. As a MMORPG, a massive online open world where thousands of players play at the same time, it is also one of the more difficult things to make. Apparently it took the author a day and roughly 2000$ in Claude tokens.

Chris Heatherly surfaced another interesting gaming-related thread: Fans are creating AI jailbreaks of games, essentially to make their own fan versions of them. Companies are starting to hunt those down but Chris argues that this is a never ending chase. Fans will continue to do this whether companies like it or not. They should just embrace modding. My belief is: Fanmade cocreation might evolve into a type of relationships businesses should be actively fostering as copying is becoming very cheap and fans will not like just being lawfared.

Now I like to talk about videogames because they are some of the more complex systems, including a codebase, art, a story and complex game mechanics. To make a fun one is an artform in itself, it involves balancing quite a few different aspects that must work well together.

I have just seen Antti Kananen, game executive and investor, state it rather plainly: building a complete game with AI is already possible; that's no longer the surprising part. Capabilities now far surpass your run-of-the-mill weird looking AI slop game, and this is only the beginning. The launch trailer of Astra 6 showcased video game making, hinting that this is something the creators of the LLM’s want to see more of. Now, as said, gaming is interesting because it is complex and difficult, all of this is true for software development and web development as well.

It is also true for marketing: It has never been easier to mass cold mail. I can’t count the amount of cold emails that are obviously following AI preselection and authoring that I receive. A scraper looks for a few “personalised” points on your website and public material and then sends you a mail that is seemingly relatively personalised. The same is true on linkedin: Contact requests that are not really based on personal interest but on automated processes. Now, I personally have never reacted to cold outreach, and this market has gotten a lot busier now.

Kaspar von Grünberg observed this change from the product site. They implemented their AI agents automatically fixing user reports, which removes communication heavy routine work entirely. Users report, the platform itself classifies the problem, light cases are fixed automatically and heavier cases essentially only need a quick review and “go”. No “please fix this”, no “can anyone help” or “we’ll forward this to the dev team” required.

What becomes scarce?

This leads us to the question I am interested in:

When making the thing itself gets cheap, fast and easy, everything that accumulated around the thing becomes relatively more important.

And this is beautifully counterintuitive. As AI emerged, everyone was worried about humans being replaced, but I am arguing that the opposite is true: The value of things that only humans can produce rises, and I think by a lot. Potentially exponentially. This concerns mainly two things: The judgement of what is actually worth pursuing, I dealt with this in an earlier article. Today we look at relationships, the human system around a product. The community that cares. My thesis is that a lot of focus will shift towards this.

The gaming examples make this very vivid. Someone made a World of Warcraft clone with AI in a night, but why should anyone care? The original game has a wildly passionate community that accumulated over years, with TV showcases such as in South Park or the Conan O’ Brian Show and countless twitch streamers that became pop icons making it an iconic cultural moment. As described in an earlier article, the game is more than just a product, but little moments such as players helping each other out for no reason that have made its playerbase care deeply.

At the same time, if gamers are really passionate, then something else happens: Mods, clones, freeshards. People modifying the product or publishing an outright clone of it. Companies decide to go after some of them, but the true compounding act might now be to embrace them, as AI makes all of this a lot cheaper, quicker and easier to produce.

I recently spoke to gaming marketing strategist Akua Harris who provided another point of view. As making a product becomes easier, people might increasingly look behind the artifact: Who made this? Why did they make this? Is this a studio or creator I actually want to support? Even if the finished products eventually become impossible to distinguish on technical quality alone, provenance, identity, reputation and intent do not suddenly become interchangeable. They may become part of why anyone chooses to care in the first place.

The same is true for any software and even marketing. When cold emailing is now done by agents and the results are, mildly spoken, of somewhat varying quality, is building actual relationships and real networks what will make a difference going forward? The product that essentially fixes itself is another vivid example. A lot of human coordination layers may now be obsolete with agents, as fixes can be implemented essentially in real time. Which relationships now become more strategically meaningful for a brand or product?

Human networks around a product or service

If we take a step back and look at a product or service in its totality, we might be able to identify a human network that is already there. People who participate around a product: They may use it, they may build with it, they might be telling their peers about it or request help. Groups may already exist, partners, maybe some marketing people organising an informal meetup that turns into something regular.

Once again an example from the gaming world: Warcraft has a massive community, each of its famous streamers got their own community and people feel like they are part of a cultural movement. Building this takes years and it does not happen in a single forum or Discord server. And I think this will become more important. It will make the difference when making the thing is now the easy part.

Can you cultivate a human network like this deliberately? And what does it mean for community work? Traditionally, and I may want to call this “legacy community”, you would set up a hosted community: a forum or container platform, or in gaming, a Discord server. These platforms will still have their place in this new world. However, we may now be moving much deeper into relationships we haven’t found particularly scalable in the past.

Who should meet? Who should do or build something together? What groups should we dedicate resources to? And why should these people care about our brand or product? Who owns the facilitation and support of these groups? I believe these will be questions that add value in the AI age. Everyone can now make a working product, so the focus shifts towards what happens after the product is already made.

The skeptical take

I have witnessed myself that some organizations might not want to invest in human networks, participation and community. The skeptical take might look something like this: “Let’s just run more paid ads, duh.” And at first, this might actually get some results. But the question remains what you are actually buying with paid ads. I doubt you are buying lasting, compounding relationships.

I think paid ads are good at buying some things but there are a lot of accumulating factors you can not cultivate with this, and here are just some examples from the gaming, SaaS and AI industries:

THE SKEPTICAL TAKE

Acquired attention vs. accumulated participation

What you can buy

  • Reach
  • Installs
  • Clicks
  • Creator placements

What has to accumulate

  • Trust
  • Reputation
  • Reusable knowledge
  • People helping each other
  • Developers building around the product
  • Shared stories and rituals
  • Advocacy

Table 1. Paid spend acquires attention; participation creates compounding value.

I would like to expand this distinction beyond “paid vs. organic”. I think this sounds more like acquired attention vs. accumulated participation. A simple question shows the difference: What happens that makes the 1001st person more likely to care? What accumulates rather than resets when spending stops?

My thesis is that this participation will change what the primary product actually is about and contains. This is increasingly looking like:

The changing role of participation

From shaping the product
to becoming part of it.

What participation used to mean

Participation
as input

  • questions shaping docs and FAQs
  • users sharing tips and workarounds
  • feedback influencing the roadmap
  • mods extending a game
  • developers building integrations
  • partners helping with implementation
  • creators sharing what they make
  • people recommending trusted products
What it is starting to mean

Participation
as production

  • answers also becoming knowledge people and agents reuse
  • tested AI workflows others can build on
  • users also building and testing solutions
  • mods also becoming whole new experiences
  • integrations also becoming tools and products
  • networks adapting solutions to different needs
  • audiences also becoming distribution channels
  • communities also building marketplaces and ecosystems

Table 2. People increasingly create part of what others experience as the product.

This means that participation (meaning community, relationships, etc.) will increasingly become part of the product itself. The question companies and brands will need to ask themselves is: How much of the value around our product will increasingly be created by people who do not work for us, and what relationship do we want with those people?

What accumulates rather than resets when spending stops?
