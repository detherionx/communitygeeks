[Richard Millington](https://mailchi.mp/feverbee/mcps2026-3575944), founder of community consultancy FeverBee, recently called MCPs (Model Context Protocol) “probably the biggest thing happening in enterprise communities right now." Within the enterprise-platform market he's writing about, that's a fair claim, and his analysis of what MCPs can and can't do is genuinely useful.

I think there is one question to ask before talking about this: What participation does an organisation need, and which structure should support it? Community management usually begins after that question has already been answered, after a platform has been chosen. A participation lens starts one step earlier.

Our working proposition is to treat the platform as one part of a wider participation system, then test its value against what people need to accomplish together.

## The MCP Argument, and Where It Stops

MCP connects AI assistants to tools and data. With a suitable community-platform integration, managers can investigate unanswered posts, retrieve knowledge and, where supported and authorised, update content. The available actions depend on the integration.

This can reduce repetitive reporting and operational work. It also requires care: Access permissions, incomplete data and unreliable calculations can limit what an assistant can tell you.

These capabilities help once an organisation has chosen an owned community platform. They do not establish whether that platform is the right structure for the participation it wants to support.

## When an Owned Community Platform Fits

An owned platform can provide a durable place for people to exchange knowledge. A support community benefits from searchable questions and answers. A complex product may need an owned knowledge environment with permissions, moderation and continuity. A customer programme may need a persistent place where relationships and contributions accumulate over time.

The strategic question is which of these requirements matters in a particular case. Start with the work participants need to do together, then choose the environment that supports it.

## Community Strategy Starts Before Platform Selection

Running an established community brings important operational questions: How healthy is it? Who participates? Which posts go unanswered? What should moderators do? How does participation contribute to support, learning or advocacy?

The participation-first approach proposed here asks what people want and need to contribute, why they would do so and how others will use the result. Those answers inform the support, continuity and governance the work needs.

The answer may be an owned community platform. It may also be a small practitioner cohort, a customer council, an event series, a developer repository, a creator network, a partner programme or several connected structures. A community platform is not the wrong answer. It is an answer that must earn its place.

This distinction is not semantic. Different forms produce different behaviour, evidence and value. A support forum is good at retaining public knowledge. A private cohort may be better for candid learning. Events create concentrated relationship opportunities. Developer environments embed participation in the work itself. Partner networks move knowledge and access across organisational boundaries.

Arcanix.AI’s Oscar Clark described community as “where players are, essentially.” Recalling Tribal Wars, he explained how his group used LINE to coordinate defence across time zones and limit infiltration. The game enabled their shared activity; a separate tool supported the coordination.

## Participation Doesn't Stop at the Platform Edge

Joe Teo of [HeyOrca](https://www.heyorca.com/), a social media management platform, put participation at the centre when explaining how involving customers helped the company make better decisions: “If we were to distill community to one word, it is participation. Without participation, there's no community.” The value comes from people helping shape what the company does. A platform can support that work, but having a platform does not establish that the work is happening.

Nor does the value of a contribution stop where it was made. Jillian Bejtlich of [Tines](https://www.tines.com/), an automation platform, described customers seeking peer knowledge through Google or an LLM rather than visiting the company website. “I’m very much building for consumption not connection,” she said. Her design priority is making community-produced knowledge useful wherever people encounter it.

Joe and Jillian prioritise different outcomes: involving people in the work, and helping people access its output. A participation strategy needs to distinguish those objectives. Who contributes? Who benefits? Do they need to be in the same space?

Katrina Hansen of [Bettermode](https://bettermode.com/), a community platform provider, described a related shift in priorities: some communities are focusing on content that could become a source for LLMs rather than pursuing member growth alone. That creates a different question for measurement. If useful knowledge reaches people outside the platform, member counts and visits cannot tell the whole story. Track where that knowledge is discovered and ask new customers whether it influenced their decision. Citations alone do not establish trust or acquisition.

The practical task is to follow the contribution through to its use. A customer raises a problem, peers contribute answers, and someone elsewhere uses that knowledge to make a decision. Those steps may happen across a forum, an event, a repository and a search result. The platform matters where it helps that process work.

AI can help connect the steps, for example, by turning an event discussion into retrievable knowledge. MCP can give an assistant access to the tools and data needed to do that. Neither decides which contributions matter, who should receive them or who must act. Those are the strategic choices the technology serves.

## Seven Questions Before Choosing a Community Platform

Before choosing a platform or extending it with MCP, an organisation should be able to answer seven questions:

1. What contribution or exchange are we trying to enable?
1. Which people need to participate, and why would they?
1. Where does this participation already happen naturally?
1. Who receives the value created, and who must act on it?
1. Does the output need to become durable, searchable knowledge?
1. What must be owned, governed or private?
1. Which combination of spaces, relationships and tools best supports that behaviour?

The answers give a platform a clear job: preserve useful knowledge, support recurring exchange, govern access or connect contributions to decisions. They also establish what success should look like beyond activity inside the platform.

## The Platform Must Earn Its Place

MCP can make a community platform easier to operate. It cannot make the case for operating it.

That case rests on what people can accomplish together: the problems they solve, the knowledge they create and the decisions they improve. Choose the structure that supports that work, and judge it by whether the work succeeds.

The community container is not the community. A better-equipped container is still no substitute for a reason to participate.
