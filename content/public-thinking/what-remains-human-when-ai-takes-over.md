What role do humans play in the age of artificial intelligence?
A question that sounds theoretical but can certainly be answered in practical terms for Communitygeeks. Of course, AI is drastically changing our work processes: much can be outsourced, but does this make humans obsolete, or is it simply shifting the focus of human work? The latter seems to be the case. We have assessed this based on our own experiences and formulated three theses:

## Thesis 1: Artificial intelligence is there to carry out tasks; judgement and ownership remain with humans

People do not necessarily have to have written every sentence in a text themselves, as AI is already capable of writing complex sentences. Human judgement is specifically required for what a text or a design asserts, conveys and says about the brand. There are good examples of this in our own experience alone.

Grammar, spelling and sentence structure are things that AI carries out reliably. But this is where it gets complex. Texts produced by AI can be better than those written by humans, as only a certain proportion of people possess the talent and ability for truly good writing. ‘Author’ has always been a distinct, recognised professional title. However, the ‘quality of execution’, an area in which language models excel, must in any case be distinguished from argumentative and epistemic ownership, which clearly belongs to humans. To complicate matters further, an AI-generated text can certainly come across as generic, as certain patterns frequently recur in the phrasing. Consequently, some texts sound very much like the work of artificial intelligence and require repeated human editing before they are polished.

Human judgement is therefore required both as a guideline and for fine-tuning. Another example of this is the design of the Communitygeeks website. Here, Carmelito drew inspiration from the ‘Bauhaus’ style, an art and design school which, incidentally, managed entirely without AI. The first Bauhaus version produced by Claude was not yet the final design. We, Katharina (editor) and Carmelito (founder), then further refined the result over several rounds. This was again carried out via Claude, but guided by our own aesthetic judgement, whereby we even drew on long-forgotten knowledge from our art lessons. Human judgement therefore comes into play not only before execution, but also afterwards, as a correction to an already ‘good’ result. The first usable result was not yet the right one for the brand and was even immediately recognised as AI-generated by designer and programmer friends. The website’s design now bears only a distant resemblance to the Bauhaus style, as further rounds of AI generation and human correction have taken place.

## Thesis 2: The human ability to verify claims as a variable in critical engagement

This thesis is the result of a philosophical conversation with Claude. When searching for the genuine role of humans, the question naturally arises as to where and when we generally rely on AI, and to what extent AI responses can be independently verified. If one asks the AI very everyday questions, for example: “Which foods are high in protein?”, one generally relies on the generated answer. Of course, one could check it. Or perhaps not? This is where it gets interesting. For the AI’s sources may themselves have been created by AI. However, there are still enough books and enough experts who acquired their knowledge before the age of artificial intelligence. One can, however, imagine that over the coming decades, knowledge will increasingly be generated by AI and thus also passed on in this way. We would then be faced with a contamination of sources and an erosion of verifiability. It becomes even more interesting when AI makes statements about itself. Claude, at least, says that there is an interpretability problem here which even the developers of current language models have so far been unable to solve.

It is therefore crucial whether a statement is, in principle, verifiable and whether humans still possess an ability to verify claims that was developed independently of AI. It makes a difference whether it is merely a lack of access to a verifiable source, whether the necessary knowledge is only just being researched, or whether the independent point of reference itself is increasingly being lost due to AI-generated content.

There is more to this philosophical discourse with AI than the surface-level outcome. Rather, this ‘conversation’ has shown that AI can be used to test one’s own ideas whilst simultaneously sharpening critical thinking. AI is thus a conversation partner that takes the pressure off the team, a team with whom one might otherwise first discuss one’s own thoughts on a topic, or perhaps even need to hold a meeting.

Nevertheless, dialogue with the team will take place in a different way: after all, people generally enjoy chatting and telling stories for the sake of storytelling, or as part of maintaining relationships. This brings us straight to Thesis 3:

## Thesis 3: Consequences are at the heart of human empathy

When considering the fundamental question ‘What will humans actually still be responsible for?’, empathy must not be overlooked simply because it sounds too obvious. However, a precise distinction needs to be made.
Current language models are good at providing empathetic responses. They can congratulate someone on a publication or wish them a lovely day. Nevertheless, in our experience, such a statement carries more weight when it comes from a human being. Why is that? In the Communitygeeks team, we want to continue working well together in the future. When we wish each other every success or congratulate one another on a publication, we know that it is meant sincerely. It would be an oversimplification to say that AI’s empathy is ‘fake’ whilst that of humans is ‘real’. But it would have consequences for our team bonds if we did not mean our good wishes or compliments sincerely. Empathy and interpersonal relationships will therefore remain relevant.

Conclusion: The real question, then, is where exactly human decisions make the difference. After all, humans are definitely needed: when it comes to determining what a result is supposed to mean; when it comes to deciding whether we can still verify a statement ourselves; and when it comes to assessing the cost of someone not being sincere. Three very different answers to the same initial question.
