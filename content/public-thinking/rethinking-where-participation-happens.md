Where does "real" community interaction fit in, in an online communication landscape that's increasingly driven by AI? Is it still relevant? These are the questions currently on our minds here at Communitygeeks. During our research, we came across the concept of ecosystems, used at Glide among others. Andy Claremont is Head of Ecosystem and Community there, and was willing to have an inspiring conversation about it.

His working definition of "ecosystem" was very illuminating for us. All groups associated with a company — technology partners, service and solution partners, affiliate and referral partners, influencers and content creators, customer advocates and ambassadors, and sponsored events — are part of the ecosystem. So this is about a wide-reaching network that nevertheless captures the spirit of community.

<blockquote class="pt-pullquote">Instead of asking which channels need to be served next, the focus shifts to the interplay between different stakeholders around a company.</blockquote>

Community doesn't become irrelevant when we treat the groups around a company as a network. We're simply thinking beyond a single interaction platform. That also means accepting that a far more complex system emerges, one that can never be fully controlled. Still, the growth of such an ecosystem can be supported.

From this conversation, a possible approach to working with such ecosystems emerges for us: Identify. Understand. Shape. Strengthen.

- First, we identify the groups that are actually significant within the ecosystem.
- We understand how they relate to one another.
- We design programs that support each group.
- We strengthen existing interactions.

The network of partners, builders, and users needs tending. In smaller ecosystems, that can still happen personally, through direct contact and occasionally on-site visits. But once an ecosystem reaches a certain size, one-on-one relationship management stops working. This is exactly where we recognize a lot from community management: methods that address a whole group at once, rather than tending each relationship individually.

Perhaps that's an interesting lead for our original question. If exchange around companies has long since moved beyond a clearly defined community, we may need to ask less about which platform "community" will happen on next. What might be more interesting is which people and groups around a company are actually connected to each other, and where community management methods can help support those relationships.

Ecosystems would then be something different: one of several ways to rethink where participation around companies is actually happening today.
