I have spent late summer and the beginning of autumn back home in Germany, in the countryside just outside Frankfurt, Wiesbaden and Mainz, where farmers sell directly from the farm this time of year.

One of the farmers here sells only on Fridays, and I stopped by for some fresh apple juice. A good number of elderly locals were there, chatting with the farmer, who was himself of higher age. I couldn't help but notice how much joy these little conversations brought the people around, as if they had become increasingly rare.

The disappearance of such third spaces, community spaces that sit between family and work, is a modern phenomenon widely observed. Shopping at a local farmer is a classic example: you interact with your neighbours, which is the smallest possible definition of a community.

I recently had a discussion with Christina Garnett, and her claim that "third spaces have disappeared" has stayed with me. I kept thinking about the people around the apple juice stand. They were still there, after all. I thought of a gym I found in Thailand and, of all places, World of Warcraft. These places still exist. They take some work to become part of, and once you are, people start expecting you back.

## Muay Thai in Thailand: carving out your own community

I have been going to Thailand to train Muay Thai since 2017. After a break, I returned in 2024 and went to Phuket, to the famous fitness street Soi Tai Ed. I researched a few gyms and was contacted by Dan from England, who invited me to the gym he trained at. At first I was just looking for a place to train. To become part of it, I had to keep coming back.

The moment I stepped inside I knew this was the Muay Thai experience I had always been looking for. The owner, Kru Rong, really wants to build the next generation of champions, and training alongside Thai fighters of all ages who treat you like family is how I always pictured training in Thailand. It is humbling to see experienced 9 year old fighters with exceptional skill and grit, and teenagers reaching for championship stars.

Many gyms talk about family but this has truly felt like being part of a team where you win and suffer together. The higher level fighters here are all down to earth and respectful too. Nobody ever pressured me to fight, and all the members have treated me as an equal.

At the end of one of my Thailand stays, I watched my friend Brendan triumph in his first professional Muay Thai fight at Bangla Boxing Stadium, against an opponent who looked more experienced than him. He seemed to be losing, but he pulled off a spinning elbow he had been practicing in his basement since childhood, and knocked his opponent out cold when we had almost written him off. Everyone from the gym was screaming. It was truly a Rocky moment.

On another night I watched Dan, the person who brought me to our gym, win his own first professional Muay Thai fight. His first Phuket coach flew in to be in his corner, and his father in law came to give him the pre-fight blessing. Watching friends go through this is stressful enough. It takes some serious huevos to be the one in the ring.

I started to care for the people in this gym and to root for them. Training was still hard, and I kept turning up. What I did almost 10.000km from home in Thailand was put myself out there to find my own community. The community, in turn, asked something of me. Back home in Germany, Vereine and other local groups are there too. You have to turn up more than once, and they will probably expect something from you before you're fully part of it. Those moments feel harder to come by now.

## Online community spaces and their reemergence: WoW Forever

I saw something similar online. Blizzard recently launched the beta of World of Warcraft: Forever, another go at reliving Classic WoW before any expansion changed and streamlined the gameplay.

The game is harder than the current version of WoW. Blizzard engineer Ana Resendez said in an [interview about WoW Forever](https://www.inven.co.kr/webzine/news/?news=320796): "The process of searching for people, communicating, and embarking on an adventure together is one of the core experiences we want to revive in Forever." The game gives you a reason to speak to strangers instead of just running past them. During one of my playthroughs, a random stranger gave me some Arcane Elixirs with nothing asked in return, and waited after the trade failed because my inventory was full. Nothing asked in return.

Youtuber Az from Heelsvsbabyface said the same thing, unprompted, in a recent video: it is "community" that makes WoW Forever so enjoyable. He described banding together to overcome difficult enemies, then the other players buffing him and bidding him farewell before leaving, feeling guilty that as a Warlock he couldn't buff them back. "A nice little moment," he said, "and the most simple of things make you think oh that was a nice little moment."

Nobbel87 is a streamer who made epic video essays about the lore, the history of Warcraft. He  started out "making videos for his guildies" but became somewhat of an authoritative source. My Youtube feed shows I mostly watched him around 12-14 years ago. He is back, and so are other people who had left the live game for years. I am watching him again and it feels rather surreal and nice to see all these people together after all this time.

I think Christina is right that places like the farmer's stand are becoming rarer. To have such third places, such communities around you, has become much less natural in today’s world. Dan invited me to the gym, but I still had to come back, train and get to know everyone. WoW Forever gives you a reason to ask another player for help instead of running past them. Az felt bad he couldn't buff other people back since he played a class who does not have those spells. He really wanted to do his small part to help others. I know the feeling of owing something, of wanting to give back. I felt it strongly in the gym in Thailand. It's a good feeling.

I still think a lot about my encounters with the elderly locals at our local farmer. The chats, the laughs. The familiarity of people speaking in our local German dialect of Hessisch. They might come here to run errands, buy some fresh apple juice or eggs. But they might also come for the conversations, the familiarity and community. I hope he keeps opening on Fridays. I think a few people count on it.
